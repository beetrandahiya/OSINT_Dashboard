Four ways to draw the satellites

All four use the same real snapshot: 283 satellites from CelesTrak's orbit data, placed where they were at the moment noted in each drawing. Nothing moves here; in the app they would. Drag the first two to turn the Earth, scroll to zoom. Pick a satellite to see each idea at a low, a medium and a high orbit.

1. Orrery

The whole system to true scale. Every orbit is a ring in space, coloured by its type, with the satellite sitting on its own ring. The three families are obvious at a glance: a green haze hugging the Earth, amber navigation rings, the blue geostationary belt.

Good for

Understanding what is up there and which orbit a satellite is in.

Costs

The Earth becomes small, so the ground is unreadable. It would be its own "Space" view, separate from the map, built in real 3D.

2. Spotlight

Close in on the Earth. The selected satellite hangs above the surface on its ring and throws a cone of light down: the glowing patch is exactly the area it can see. Other satellites fade back. For a geostationary satellite the cone covers almost half the planet.

Good for

"What is this one looking over right now?" The view you described.

Costs

Needs the same real-3D view as the orrery. High orbits leave the frame unless you zoom out.

3. Look down

You are at the satellite's own position, looking straight down. The curved edge is its real horizon. Low satellites see a region; a geostationary one sees a whole face of the Earth. Readouts give height, speed and orbit type.

Good for

The "put me at that point" view. Works with the satellite photo layer as ground.

Costs

It is geometry, not a camera feed: what an on-board camera points at is not published. The ground would be our map or yesterday's photo.

4. Height ladder

A strip for the satellite's card: every satellite as a tick at its height, on a scale that gives the crowded low orbits room. The selected one is marked, and the bands name the orbit type.

Good for

Showing orbit type visually inside the card or along the edge of the map. Cheap; works with the map we have today.

Costs

It says how high, not where.
