World Watch · identity, round two · 28 Nov 2026

Six globes, each drawn from a fact

You asked for a globe that is special and not generic. Every mark below is computed, not sketched: a real orbit at true scale, what a real satellite sees over Munich, the tiles the map engine loads, the land in view from above home. Each comes as a small identity: its colours from a real agency (with the source), a wordmark, two graphic devices for the app, the top bar and a splash screen, and an honest verdict.

Drawn first and dropped after the 16 px test: the usual wireframe globe with a grid (generic), two day-and-night globes (they read as the moon or a dark-mode switch), a polar clock (a gauge), a globe with only Munich's meridian and parallel (a trident), and Goode's interrupted map, whose southern edge really does form VVVV but reads as teeth. Round one, ten directions without a globe: the first brand book.

First choice · palette: NATO

Oko

The orbit of the Soviet early-warning satellites called Oko, “eye”, drawn to scale with the Earth: it looks like an eye because it was one.

Oko is Russian for eye. From 1972 the Soviet Union watched for missile launches with infrared satellites on Molniya orbits: long ellipses that swing a satellite low past the Earth and then hold it high over the northern hemisphere for most of its twelve hours.

Drawn to scale, with the Earth where physics puts it (at one focus), the orbit becomes an eye and the planet its iris, almost touching the line at the near end. In large sizes the line can give way to the satellite's position every 30 minutes: the dots bunch at the far end, where it slows down and watches.

What works

The strongest story of the set, and every part of it is true: the shape, the scale and the name. Nothing like the usual globe icons; there is an eye in it without anyone drawing an eye. Clean at 16 px.

What does not

At small size it sits close to the generic “orbit” icon (an oval around a planet); the meaning needs the story once. It quotes a Soviet military programme: flavour for a private tool, but say so if that bothers you. The Earth is a plain disc, as there is no room for coastlines at this scale.

Second choice · palette: NGA (National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency)

Unrolled

At Greenwich the globe unrolls into the square tiles the map engine really loads; the one lit tile holds Munich.

World Watch is one world drawn two ways, and the switch between globe and flat map was the first decision in the project. The mark shows both at once.

West of Greenwich the Earth is a globe with curved meridians. East of it the same world becomes what the map engine actually downloads: square Web Mercator tiles, four by four at zoom 2. The teal tile is the one that contains Munich.

Third choice · palette: Palantir (Blueprint)

Footprint

Everything a satellite can see from above Munich, drawn on the globe: the Earth becomes an eyeball and the footprint its iris.

A satellite sees everything inside a circle on the globe, and the size of that circle depends only on its height. For Sentinel-2, whose pictures the app lists under “photographed from orbit”, the circle reaches 27.1 degrees from the point below it: 6,030 km across.

Centred on Munich and seen from off to one side, the circle becomes an ellipse and the globe an eye looking at home. The app already draws this shape as the satellite spotlight.

Also drawn · palette: Bundesregierung (German Federal Government)

Above home

The Earth seen from straight above Munich, which shows almost as much land as any single view of the Earth can.

Look down on the Earth from far above Munich and almost half of what you see is land: 45.5 percent, with four fifths of all the world's land in one view. No view does much better. The best, centred over western France, reaches 46.7 percent; geographers call it the land hemisphere, and home is about 10 degrees from its centre.

The mark is that view, with the real coastlines and a point on Munich.

Also drawn · palette: EUMETSAT

Octant

A triangle with three right angles: impossible on paper, ordinary on a globe, and Munich is inside it.

Draw a line from the North Pole down the Greenwich meridian to the equator, a quarter of the way round the equator, and back up to the pole. Every corner is a right angle, the angles add up to 270 degrees, and the triangle covers exactly one eighth of the Earth.

Munich lies inside it. A flat map cannot draw this shape truthfully, which is why World Watch has a globe.

Also drawn · palette: DLR (German Aerospace Center)

Lune

Home's hour on the globe: the 15-degree slice of Central European Time, a shape with only two sides.

The Earth turns 15 degrees an hour, and time zones were laid out as 15-degree slices from pole to pole. Germany's hour, UTC+1, is nominally the slice from 7.5 to 22.5 degrees east, and Munich is inside it.

On a sphere such a slice is a lune, a figure with two sides that cannot exist on a flat page. Face on, it is a slit across the globe: an hour, a watch, an eye half closed.

What works

Very simple, and it ties “watch” to time. DLR, whose space operations centre is at Oberpfaffenhofen near Munich, is a local and fitting palette.

What does not

The weakest at small size: a disc with a vertical slit reads as a coin on edge, a reptile's eye or a power symbol. Real time zones zigzag; the lune is the nominal one.

Recommendation

My pick

  1. Oko

    The one with a story worth telling and no resemblance to other globe logos. It is about watching from space, it is exact, and it stays clean at taskbar size. If the Soviet name bothers you, the same orbit was also flown by American early-warning and signals satellites; the shape works without the name.

  2. Unrolled

    The most "World Watch" in the literal sense: globe and map, the two views the app has. Worth keeping as the Map / Globe switch inside the app whichever logo you choose.

  3. Footprint

    The quickest to read: a globe that is an eye. The risk is that it looks like a cartoon eye.

Above home is the safe choice if you want people to see the Earth at once. Octant and Lune are clever but weaker at small size.

What was checked and what was not

Marks were rendered in Chromium at one device pixel per pixel; not in Safari, not on your real taskbar or Dock. Windows scales icons with its own filter and macOS adds its own mask and glass, so final icons need checking there. Facts: horizon angles, the Molniya period and its time in the far half, the land shares and the tile numbers were computed for this page (the land from the app's own Natural Earth outlines). The Molniya heights are typical values; real orbits vary. NATO, Blueprint, NGA and Bundesregierung colours were read from their sources in round one; EUMETSAT and DLR colours come from their websites' stylesheets on 28 Nov 2026 and are not brand guides.
